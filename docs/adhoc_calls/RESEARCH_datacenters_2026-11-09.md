# Research note — data-centre build-out, the new Congress, and the memory supply agreements (2026-11-09, 09:00 UTC)

Asked by the user on the morning after the Nvidia CEO investigation and six days after the midterms. Tags: M = read from a primary document or an exchange/perp price; S = press or tracker, not independently verified; D = derived; H = my inference.

## 1. What has actually been cancelled or blocked
- Data Center Watch (S, its own pages): Q1 2026 "at least 75 projects worth approximately $130 billion" blocked or delayed; Q2 2026 "at least 45 projects worth $68 billion", research window April-June 2026. Blocked and delayed are not separated and not defined; no gigawatt totals; no hyperscaler project named; the project list is behind a paywall. Regulatory scrutiny "spans 49 states"; "thirty statehouses introduced, and many adopted" siting, electricity, water or cost-sharing measures.
- Carbon Direct via Route Fifty (S): 46 (elsewhere 49) AI data-centre projects in 20 states, $170 B, "publicly delayed or cancelled after community opposition", January 2024 to May 2026; the most common cause named is the lack of transparency about the end user — projects with undisclosed tenants face the fastest opposition.
- S&P Global (S, one line): more than 146 delayed or cancelled through early August 2026, three quarters tied to local opposition.
- The one hyperscaler-grade cancellation found (S, several outlets): Oracle and OpenAI dropped the expansion of the Abilene Stargate campus from ~1.2 GW to 2 GW on financing and demand forecasts; the capacity was re-leased — Microsoft ~700 MW via Crusoe. Stargate's New Mexico campus flagged for a power-permit delay. SemiAnalysis (S) argues the "half of 2026 capacity slips" claim is overstated: its year-end 2026 hyperscaler self-build forecast moved ~1 %, colocation under 5 %.
- Reading (D): the blocked dollars are overwhelmingly developer and speculative projects at the zoning or permitting stage, counted at announced value. No named hyperscaler-owned campus has been cancelled for political reasons in the sources read. The constraint is power and permits, which moves revenue to the right; it has not yet removed demand.

## 2. The political set-up after Nov 3
- Congress (S, AP via several outlets): Democrats won both chambers — Senate 54-44 with two races uncalled at the time of the count, House about 254-162 with 19 uncalled. They take over in January.
- States (S, Bloomberg via the Spokesman-Review, Nov 8, citing Cleanview): about 40 % of pending US data-centre capacity sits in states whose governors-elect campaigned to stop or pause construction (Ohio, Georgia, Michigan, New Mexico, Texas) or to prolong existing pauses (New York, Oregon). 46 local ballot measures approved or on track, at least 17 outright bans on large data centres, nearly half in Ohio. Polling: Quinnipiac, nearly three in four oppose a data centre in their community; an Ohio poll, 57 % would vote for a total ban. Bloomberg Intelligence estimate: a 5-7 % cut to the "$1.4 trillion" 2027 AI infrastructure spend and up to 10 GW not online in 2027 as planned.
- Federal agenda (S, Reuters via US News, Nov 6): Democrats plan bills on consumer electricity costs from data centres, independent testing of advanced AI systems, and AI-company liability; the incoming Senate majority leader named ratepayer protection a priority. The Republican Ratepayer Protection Act failed 57-43 in September; the Democratic alternative on file (GRID Savings Act) would give FERC rulemaking authority over loads of 150 MW or more and make those loads pay the grid costs they cause. No federal moratorium proposal was found.
- How hard is federal action (D): a 54-seat Senate majority is six short of cloture, so a statutory moratorium or a federal siting regime is improbable; cost-allocation and disclosure mandates that attract a handful of Republican votes are the realistic ceiling, and reconciliation cannot carry a siting rule. The fast levers are elsewhere: governors' executive orders, state public-utility commissions, county zoning, and FERC — none of which waits for January. Export legislation is the exception: bills to restrict H200/Blackwell exports already exist with bipartisan sponsors and the CEO matter adds votes.

## 4. The memory supply agreements and the "LTA exit" thesis
- Micron (M, FY26 results 8-K, Sept 30, 2026): "Proceeds from customer contract liability deposits $12,747 M" in fiscal 2026; noncurrent customer contract liabilities $12,895 M at year end against $568 M a year earlier; capex $27.4 B; "record fiscal 2027" expected. Press (S): 26 strategic customer agreements covering about 35 % of revenue through 2030, with take-or-pay terms under which the customer pays whether or not it takes delivery; prepayments of 10-30 % of contract value against a historical norm under 5 %.
- SK hynix (S): agreements with 14 customers covering more than half of 2027-28 capacity; price bands, minimums and cancellation terms not disclosed. Samsung and SK hynix "sold out" for 2027 HBM is second-hand.
- Reading (D): the user's chain — cancellations → customers exit LTAs → drag on memory and semis — has the risk sitting with the wrong party in its first link. Cash has moved from customer to supplier: a hyperscaler that walks forfeits deposits and still owes the minimum. The agreements' weak points are different: (a) the counterparties under them are the hyperscalers and the GPU vendor, whose capex is rising, not the blocked developers; (b) the real exposure is 2028 pricing when the prepaid volumes land in a market with more supply; (c) the China channel — an export restriction on the leader's China-bound parts takes HBM volumes with it, which is why Korea's perps were the weakest market this morning (SKHYNIX −5 %, Samsung −3 %, EWY −3 %) while AMD and the equipment names were up. The LTA thesis becomes live only if a hyperscaler cuts capex and says so; the first observable would be a hyperscaler citing policy risk in its capex language, then a deposit write-off at a supplier.

## 5. What would change this read
A named hyperscaler campus cancelled for siting or policy reasons; a hyperscaler guiding 2027 capex down; a supplier disclosing a customer default or a deposit reversal; a federal siting or moratorium bill with 60 Senate sponsors; or the 40 % of pending capacity in pause-states turning into gubernatorial orders in Ohio or Texas in January. Any one of those moves the chain from political to financial.
